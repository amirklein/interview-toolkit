# Rubric Template

Copy this file to `<your-discipline>.md` in the same folder and fill it in. Every skill in the
toolkit that evaluates work — `assignment-framing`, `assignment-evaluator`, `assignment-defense` —
reads whichever rubric matches the discipline in `~/.interview-toolkit/profile.md`.

A rubric answers four questions: what bar is being cleared, what dimensions the work is judged on,
what a strong response contains, and who's in the room when it's defended.

Keep dimensions to four or six. More than six and nothing gets checked properly; fewer than four
and you're not covering the ground.

Write in second person ("your solution section…"), because the skills reading this are speaking
to the person.

---

## The bar

One sentence naming the standard the work has to clear. Not "is this good" — a specific person
having a specific reaction.

> Example, from `product.md`: would a CPTO at a serious company trust this person to own a
> product line without hand-holding?

This sentence does a lot of work. Every skill runs its judgment through it, so make it concrete
enough to be uncomfortable.

## Dimensions

Four to six. Each one needs all three parts — a dimension without a "common risk" is a checklist
item, not a rubric entry.

### Dimension N: [Name]

**What strong looks like**: the reaction a reviewer has when this dimension is handled well.
Describe the finished impression, not the activity.

**Evaluate**:
- Three to five direct questions to ask of the work
- Phrase them so they can be answered yes or no against a real draft
- Concrete over abstract: "could an engineer start a sprint from this?" beats "is it detailed?"

**Common risk**: the specific way capable people lose points here. This is the most valuable part
of the entry, and the hardest to write — it should name a failure that looks fine on the surface.

## What a strong response contains

The sections a strong deliverable in this discipline usually has, each with the trap that most
often catches people. `assignment-framing` builds its attack plan from this list, so order it the
way the work should be ordered.

**Section: [Name]**
- What it covers
- The depth expected
- **Common trap**: the specific mistake, not a generic warning

Note which sections are load-bearing and which are optional depending on the brief. An attack plan
that treats all sections as mandatory produces a twenty-hour response to a four-hour assignment.

## The panel

Who's in the room when this work gets presented, and what each one presses on.
`assignment-defense` plays these roles.

**[Role]** — what they care about, and the question they'll open with.

Three or four is enough. Include at least one person whose interests the work threatens — the
adversarial voice is where most defenses actually fall apart.

## Calibration note

State honestly where this rubric came from: real feedback and lived experience, or general
knowledge of the discipline. Anyone reading it deserves to know how much weight it carries, and
the skills will pass the caveat along if asked.
