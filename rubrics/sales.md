# Rubric — Sales

**Calibration**: uncalibrated. A reasonable starting point based on general knowledge of what sales
exercises — mock discovery calls, territory plans, deal strategy write-ups, mock pitches — get
judged on. Not lived feedback the way `product.md` is. Edit it to match what you've actually been
told.

---

## The bar

Would a VP of Sales put you in front of their largest at-risk account next week without sitting in?

---

## Dimensions

### Dimension 1: Business outcome over product

**What strong looks like**: everything ties to a number the buyer already cares about. Features
appear only as the mechanism for an outcome, never as the point.

**Evaluate**:
- Is there a quantified business case, even estimated?
- Does the value statement use the buyer's metrics, or the product's vocabulary?
- Is the cost of inaction made concrete?
- Would the economic buyer recognize their own priorities in this?

**Common risk**: a fluent, enthusiastic feature tour. It demos well and closes nothing, because
nobody signs to acquire capabilities.

### Dimension 2: The decision-making unit

**What strong looks like**: every person who can say yes or no is identified, along with what each
one needs and who's likely to block. There's a plan for reaching the buyer you don't yet have
access to.

**Evaluate**:
- Are champion, economic buyer, technical evaluator, and likely blocker all named?
- Is there a stated path to the people not yet in the conversation?
- Are the internal politics acknowledged — who loses if this happens?
- Is procurement, security review, or legal accounted for in the timeline?

**Common risk**: running a great process with one enthusiastic champion who turns out not to control
budget. The deal dies in a quarter you'd already forecast.

### Dimension 3: Discovery and qualification

**What strong looks like**: real questions that would surface real problems, and a willingness to
disqualify. The exercise shows curiosity before it shows a solution.

**Evaluate**:
- Are the questions open and specific, or leading?
- Does the pain get quantified, or just described?
- Is there a compelling event, and is it the buyer's or invented?
- Is there a stated basis for walking away?

**Common risk**: discovery as a formality before a predetermined pitch. Experienced buyers feel it
immediately, and evaluators are testing for exactly that.

### Dimension 4: Objection handling

**What strong looks like**: objections are surfaced early and resolved on their merits, including
the ones nobody raised out loud. Price pressure is handled by re-establishing value, not by
discounting reflexively.

**Evaluate**:
- Are the three hardest objections named before the buyer raises them?
- Is each one actually answered, or absorbed and moved past?
- Is the competitive comparison honest about where you lose?
- Is there a response to "we'll do nothing" — usually the real competitor?

**Common risk**: acknowledging an objection warmly and continuing as though it were resolved. It
resurfaces during internal review, when you're not in the room to answer it.

### Dimension 5: Command of the close

**What strong looks like**: a mutual plan with dates, owners, and a next step that's already
scheduled. Urgency comes from the buyer's timeline, not from a quarter-end discount.

**Evaluate**:
- Is there a specific next step with a date, or a vague follow-up?
- Is there a mutual action plan working backward from the buyer's deadline?
- Is the urgency real and theirs?
- Is there an ask, made plainly, without apology?

**Common risk**: an excellent conversation that ends in "I'll send over some materials." The
momentum was real and it wasn't converted into a commitment.

## What a strong response contains

**1. Account and buyer context** *(load-bearing)* — who they are, what's happening in their business
right now, why this matters this quarter. **Common trap**: reciting their website back to them.

**2. The problem and its cost** *(load-bearing)* — what's broken, quantified. **Common trap**: pain
described adjectivally rather than numerically.

**3. Stakeholder map** *(load-bearing)* — everyone who matters, what each needs. **Common trap**:
champion only.

**4. The recommendation** — what you're proposing, framed as an outcome. **Common trap**: a feature
list with a price at the bottom.

**5. The business case** — value against cost, with your assumptions stated. **Common trap**: ROI
built on numbers the buyer would immediately dispute.

**6. Objections and competitive position** — the hard ones, answered. **Common trap**: pretending
you win everywhere.

**7. Mutual plan and close** *(load-bearing)* — dates, owners, next step. **Common trap**: ending
without an ask.

## The panel

**The VP of Sales** — cares whether you can be trusted with real revenue. Opens with: "What's your
read on whether this deal is actually winnable?"

**The buyer persona** — cares about their own problem and their own risk. Presses on: "Why should I
do this now rather than next year?"

**The skeptical procurement or security voice** — cares about risk and precedent. Presses on: "Who
else in our industry has done this, and what happened?"

**The internal blocker** — the person whose current solution or budget this displaces. Presses on:
"We already bought something for this. Why was that wrong?"
