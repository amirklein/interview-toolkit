# Rubric — Support / Customer Success

**Calibration**: uncalibrated. A reasonable starting point based on general knowledge of what
support and CS exercises — mock ticket responses, escalation write-ups, at-risk account plans,
QBR decks — get judged on. Not lived feedback the way `product.md` is. Edit it to match what you've
actually been told.

---

## The bar

Would a head of CS give you their most difficult, highest-value account and stop checking in on it?

---

## Dimensions

### Dimension 1: Resolution quality

**What strong looks like**: the customer's actual problem is solved, not just the question they
asked. The response is accurate, complete, and written so they don't need to come back.

**Evaluate**:
- Does it address what they need, or only what they literally asked?
- Is it technically correct, and does it avoid guessing where it should verify?
- Are next steps and expectations explicit — what happens now, by when, from whom?
- Would this generate a follow-up ticket?

**Common risk**: an answer that's correct and useless. It closes the ticket and the underlying
problem shows up again next week under a different subject line.

### Dimension 2: Root cause and systemic thinking

**What strong looks like**: the individual issue is connected to the pattern behind it, and there's a
concrete recommendation aimed at the cause — a product fix, a doc gap, an onboarding change.

**Evaluate**:
- Is the cause identified, or just the symptom?
- Is this issue connected to others like it?
- Is there a specific, actionable recommendation to someone outside support?
- Is the volume or cost of the pattern quantified?

**Common risk**: excellent individual resolutions with no aggregation. The person is valuable and
unpromotable, because nothing they do reduces future volume.

### Dimension 3: Communication under pressure

**What strong looks like**: calm, clear, and appropriately accountable. Ownership without
over-apologizing, honesty about what went wrong, and a tone matched to how angry the customer
actually is.

**Evaluate**:
- Does the tone match the severity, or is it templated?
- Is there ownership without excessive apology or blame-shifting?
- Is bad news delivered plainly and early, or buried?
- Is jargon translated for the audience?

**Common risk**: two failure modes, both common. Over-apologizing reads as weakness and invites
escalation; defending the product reads as not listening. The middle — "here's what happened,
here's what we're doing" — is harder and rarer.

### Dimension 4: Proactive risk management

**What strong looks like**: risk is named before it becomes churn. Health signals are read, and
there's an intervention plan with a timeline rather than a reaction.

**Evaluate**:
- Are the leading indicators identified — usage decline, champion departure, support volume,
  unanswered exec sponsor?
- Is there a specific intervention with an owner and a date?
- Is renewal risk quantified and forecast honestly, including the bad news?
- Is expansion opportunity identified alongside the risk?

**Common risk**: reactive excellence. Every fire gets handled beautifully, and nobody notices the
account was preventable three months earlier.

### Dimension 5: Boundaries and internal advocacy

**What strong looks like**: says no to unreasonable asks without damaging the relationship, and
pushes internally on behalf of the customer with a case rather than a complaint.

**Evaluate**:
- Are commitments made only where they can be kept?
- Is an unreasonable ask redirected to what's actually possible?
- When advocating internally, is there a business case — revenue at risk, accounts affected?
- Are expectations reset early rather than after a missed date?

**Common risk**: promising a roadmap item to save a conversation. It buys a quarter and costs the
relationship permanently when it doesn't ship.

## What a strong response contains

**1. Situation summary** *(load-bearing)* — what's happening, how severe, who's affected. **Common
trap**: restating the ticket instead of assessing it.

**2. Immediate response** *(load-bearing)* — what the customer gets right now, and when. **Common
trap**: investigating before acknowledging.

**3. Root cause** *(load-bearing)* — why this happened. **Common trap**: stopping at the proximate
cause.

**4. The pattern** — how often this happens and what it costs. **Common trap**: treating the case as
unique when it isn't.

**5. Internal recommendation** — what product, engineering, or docs should change, with the case for
it. **Common trap**: a complaint rather than a proposal.

**6. Risk assessment** — where the relationship stands and what could still go wrong. **Common
trap**: optimism that reads as not paying attention.

**7. Follow-through plan** — owners and dates. **Common trap**: ending at resolution with no
verification that it held.

## The panel

**The head of CS** — cares whether you can be trusted with the hardest accounts. Opens with: "What
would you have done differently three months before this blew up?"

**The angry customer** — cares about their own outage and their own credibility internally. Presses
on: "This is the third time. Why should I believe you now?"

**The product manager** — cares whether your escalation is worth reprioritizing for. Presses on:
"How many accounts is this, and what's it worth?"

**The engineer** — cares whether the report is actionable. Presses on: "What exactly did you try,
and what did you see?"
