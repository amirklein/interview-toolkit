How it works

Brief on Monday. Call on Friday.

Six skills covering the whole arc, from decoding the brief to surviving the follow-up. Type / in your agent and they're there. Use the ones you need.

FIRST, ONCE /profile-builder

A ten-minute interview covering what you've actually done and where your skill doesn't yet show up on paper. Everything else reads it. Without it the toolkit is generic; with it, it knows your patterns.

STEP 1 /assignment-framing

Reads the brief, researches the company live, names what's really being tested, and allocates depth against the hours you actually have — so a four-hour brief doesn't get a twenty-hour plan.

THROUGHOUT /thought-partner

The colleague who won't let you gloss. Pushes when the thinking is thin, develops it when it's good, and tells you when your own experience belongs on the page and isn't there.

IF ASKED FOR /prototype-builder

Researches the real product first — palette, patterns, tone — then builds a mockup that looks like it belongs inside it, with the narrative you'll use to present it.

BEFORE YOU SEND /assignment-evaluator

An honest read against the bar for your field, plus a cold-read mode that deliberately forgets the conversation and grades it like a stranger with forty minutes and four other submissions.

BEFORE THE CALL /assignment-defense

The mock panel. Hiring manager, skeptical engineer, and the person whose territory this steps on — one at a time, pressing twice, because a rehearsal that's easier than the real thing costs you.

What it's actually like

The pushback is the product.

Any model will tell you your draft looks strong. That's the problem. These skills are built to do the opposite, because the reviewer will.

assignment-evaluator — cold read
AI
Borderline

I have no history with this document. Reading it the way they will:

  • Section 3 names the feature and never defines the inputs, the failure case, or the fallback. An engineer can't start from this.
  • No pivot triggers anywhere. This is a timeline, not a plan.
  • Strong problem framing. Genuinely — the second paragraph is the best thing here.

Now comparing against your profile: your documented primary gap showed up to a reader who wasn't looking for it. That's the whole finding. It's live, not historical.

assignment-defense — panel
AI

Skeptical engineer: How long does this take to build, and what did you assume about our architecture?

YOU

Maybe a quarter? Though if that's off I could scope it down…

AI

Skeptical engineer: A quarter is optimistic. You know we're on a monolith?

YOU

AI

Stepping out of role. You just gave up a defensible position because I sounded confident — I gave you no argument, only a tone. Your original phasing was fine. Say it again, and this time hold it.

Make it yours

Two files, both yours to edit.

Everything personal lives in ~/.interview-toolkit, outside the repo. Nothing you write there is tracked by git, sent anywhere, or overwritten when you update.

~/.interview-toolkit/profile.md
## Evidence: what you've actually done - Domains you know cold: usage-based billing, developer onboarding - Shipped: replaced manual quoting with self-serve checkout (9 mo, 4 eng) - Unfair advantage: five years in ops before you moved into product ## Gaps: where it isn't visible yet Primary: your solution sections read like vision docs. An engineer can't pull three sprint tickets out of them. ## Behavioral pattern In 1:1 feedback you soften a position before you've been out-argued. → So: push once more before accepting agreement as resolution.

Written by /profile-builder from a short interview. Half of it is what you're good at — because a toolkit that only knows your weaknesses can help you write a competent answer, and never your answer.

The rubric decides the bar

Each discipline gets a rubric file: the standard being cleared, the dimensions the work is judged on, what a strong response contains, and who's in the room when you defend it. The skills read it instead of hard-coding one job's idea of good.

  • Product ships calibrated from real interview feedback.
  • Design, engineering, sales, support ship as honest starting points, labeled as such rather than pretending.
  • Anything else is one file. Copy the template, set one line in your profile, done.

Editing them is the point, not an advanced feature. Change the bar to the one you're actually held to. Delete a dimension nobody grades you on. Replace the panel with the names of the people who'll really be on your call — that one change makes the defense rehearsal dramatically better.

Install

One command.

Installs into whichever tools it finds — Claude Code, Cursor, Codex — and seeds the rubrics it will never overwrite again. 

$ curl -fsSL https://raw.githubusercontent.com/amirklein/interview-toolkit/main/install.sh | bash

Or from a clone, if you'd rather read it first:

$ git clone https://github.com/amirklein/interview-toolkit
$ cd interview-toolkit
$ ./install.sh

Then, in your agent:

> /profile-builder

After that

  • Restart your editor — a fresh window, not just a new chat, the first time you add a skills directory. Then type / to check the six are listed.
  • Run /profile-builder first. Ten minutes, and it's the difference between generic advice and advice that knows your patterns.
  • The rest work without it, falling back to general guidance. They just don't know you yet.

Other options

  • ./install.sh -t cursor,codex -y to pick tools without prompts.
  • ./install.sh -p to install into one project instead of your home directory.
  • ./install.sh --update to pull and reinstall. Your profile and edited rubrics survive it.
  • ./install.sh --uninstall to remove the skills. It leaves your profile alone.

Using Claude.ai or Claude Desktop instead? Turn on file creation in settings, upload each skill folder under Customize → Skills, and paste your profile into the chat when you start. Details are in the README.

Honestly

What this is and isn't.

It won't

  • Write your assignment. You'd have to defend it on a call, and you'd lose.
  • Tell you it's great. That's what every other chat window is for.
  • Get you past a bar you can't actually clear.
  • Replace doing the thinking. It makes the thinking harder, on purpose.

It will

  • Argue with you before a stranger gets the chance.
  • Notice when you've left your best material out.
  • Tell you what a reviewer will think, with the passage that caused it.
  • Make you say it out loud under pressure while it's still a rehearsal.

Questions

Reasonable things to ask.

Isn't this just cheating?

If you use it to have a model write your assignment, yes, and it will cost you on the follow-up call, which is where every claim in your document becomes a question. The skills are built the other way around — the evaluator hunts for anything you can't defend, and the defense skill makes you defend it out loud.

What it's for is narrower and more honest: most people don't lose these because they lack the skill. They lose because a week of pressure, a blank page, and no one to argue with produces work that's thinner than they are.

I'm not a product manager. Does it work for me?

The method is discipline-neutral and the standards are a file you control. Product ships calibrated from real feedback; design, engineering, sales, and support ship as honest starting points that say so. Anything else is one file away — copy the template, set one line in your profile.

Where does my data go?

Nowhere. Your profile is a markdown file in your home directory, read by the agent you already use and already trust with your work. There's no account, no service, and nothing here that phones home. Uninstalling doesn't touch it.

Why does the profile ask what I'm good at?

Because the failure mode of AI-assisted assignment work is competence without a fingerprint — technically fine, and indistinguishable from every other submission. The toolkit needs to know your domains and your war stories to say "this section should be the migration you actually ran, not a generic risk list."

Do I have to run the questionnaire?

No. Everything works unpersonalized and says so once. But the sharpest thing these skills do is catch your own documented pattern before a reviewer does, and they can't do that until they know what it is.

Can I change how hard it pushes?

Yes, and the interesting version of that question is whether you should. The one thing worth keeping is the double push: when you concede a point, it asks once more. If you gave in because the argument was better, that's an update, and it says so. If you gave in because it sounded confident, you just found something out about yourself that's worth more than the point.
